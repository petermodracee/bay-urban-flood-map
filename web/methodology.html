Subgrid 2D Compound Pluvial–Coastal Flood Modeling and Multi-Tier Validation for San Francisco

Version 0.6.1 · 24 Nov 2026

Abstract. Existing San Francisco Bay Area flood maps treat coastal inundation and riverine floodplains separately, leaving localized urban stormwater ponding underrepresented, while fragmented storm-drain records across 101 municipalities preclude a uniform regional 1D–2D pipe model. This document describes the v0.6.1 formulation and multi-tier validation of the SF Bay Urban Flood Map, an open-source 2D rain-on-grid screening model built on HydroMT-SFINCS. A 20 m local-inertial grid is paired with 2 m hydro-enforced USGS 3DEP LiDAR subgrid tables, four-gauge NOAA tidal boundaries, 1 km NOAA MRMS precipitation, and a uniform 5.0 mm/hr drainage abstraction alongside Soil Conservation Service (SCS) Curve Number infiltration. The model simulates coincident 10-year and 100-year rainfall plus high-tide/surge stress scenarios across 0 to +5 ft sea level rise (undefined joint return period) alongside five historical atmospheric river (AR) hindcasts. One-factor-at-a-time calibration ablations rejected wet antecedent soil moisture shifts (AMC-III) and mean-rate-matched imperviousness drainage scaling (Tier 1a/1b). On the two untouched holdout storms using right-of-way controls, 25 m capture reaches 57.09% (1.524× lift, 0.6125 ROC-AUC) on the heavy November 2024 AR (536 DataSF 311 reports) versus 0.71% (0.373× lift, 0.5057 ROC-AUC) on the moderate February 2024 AR (141 reports), with 0.0013–0.0019 m boundary-gauge RMSE.

Introduction

Compound flooding in urbanized estuaries results from the interaction of intense rainfall, rapid runoff across impervious streets, and elevated coastal water levels that restrict gravity-fed storm outfalls (Leijnse et al., 2021; Nederhoff et al., 2021). In the San Francisco Bay Area, public hazard maps leave a practical gap between coastal and inland risk: static shoreline SLR viewers omit overland rainfall accumulation, while national-scale FEMA Flood Insurance Rate Maps miss much of the localized pluvial ponding that occurs in urban depressions (Sanders et al., 2023; Schubert et al., 2024).

Building a coupled 1D–2D storm-sewer model across all nine Bay Area counties is currently blocked by data fragmentation: subsurface pipe networks are split across 101 municipalities and nine county flood-control districts with inconsistent GIS schemas and legacy CAD or PDF drawings. As in other regional 2D flood frameworks where uniform 1D pipe-invert data are unavailable, urban drainage is parameterized at the grid scale using a design-standard abstraction approach (Wing et al., 2017; cf. Bates et al., 2021, who treat pluvial and coastal hazard as separate non-interacting layers). Simple terrain fill-sink methods avoid pipe data but produce false positives behind uncarved highway overpasses and ignore flow timing and tidal backwater. The SF Bay Urban Flood Map instead uses a standalone 2D local-inertial subgrid model, Deltares Super-Fast INundation of CoastS (SFINCS), that runs locally and exports static Cloud Optimized GeoTIFFs (COGs) for browser-based viewing (Bates et al., 2010; Leijnse et al., 2021; Eilander et al., 2023; van Ormondt et al., 2025).

Simulations use fixed sea-level increments (0, +1, +3, and +5 ft) mapped dynamically in metadata to 2024 California Ocean Protection Council (OPC) projections (California Ocean Protection Council, 2024) so raster outputs stay valid as state planning horizons change. Development and validation begin on San Francisco's combined-sewer core (v0.1 through v0.6) to verify hydro-enforcement and multi-tier scoring before testing uncalibrated spatial transfer on a Municipal Separate Storm Sewer System (MS4) creek basin (San Francisquito Creek and East Palo Alto in v0.7). Each candidate physics change is tested through controlled one-factor-at-a-time ablations and temporal holdouts before adoption (Table 1).

Methods

Study Domain, Datums & Terrain Hydro-Enforcement

All terrain preprocessing, hydrodynamic simulations, and validation metrics operate strictly in metric units under EPSG:26910 (North American Datum of 1983 / Universal Transverse Mercator Zone 10N) and the North American Vertical Datum of 1988 (NAVD88). Outputs are reprojected to EPSG:3857 (Web Mercator) COGs only at final web export, with depths shallower than 0.05 m (2 in) masked as NoData. Starting from the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) 1 m LiDAR Digital Elevation Model (DEM; CA_SanFrancisco_B23) resampled to 2 m subgrid resolution, pipeline/01_prep_terrain.py applies four deterministic preprocessing steps:

  1. Hybrid building block-out & resistance (adapted from Schubert & Sanders, 2012). DataSF LiDAR building footprints (dataset ynuv-fyni) are raised by +3.0 m with Manning's roughness n = 0.30, combining the building-block and building-resistance treatments evaluated in Schubert & Sanders (2012) to prevent artificial ponding inside structures and preserve street-corridor conveyance.
  2. Bounded underpass hydro-enforcement. OpenStreetMap road and rail segments tagged as bridges, tunnels, or elevated layers are linearly carved between approach elevation medians (min_bump_m = 0.15 m, carve_half_width_m = 5.0 m). Segments with elevation offsets exceeding 6.0 m are skipped so bored hillside tunnels (such as the Broadway and Stockton tunnels) are not cut into open trenches. Reproducible YAML overrides (carve_exclude_osm_ids for the Winston Drive / Stonestown Galleria skybridges and manual_underpass_cuts) eliminate localized carving artifacts without manual raster editing.
  3. Upward-only shoreline crest enforcement. Engineered coastal defenses from the San Francisco Estuary Institute (SFEI) SF Bay Shore Inventory (levees, seawalls, floodwalls, and berms) are burned upward-only via z = max(z, Zmean) across a ±2.0 m half-width so narrow shoreline crests survive subgrid aggregation.
  4. Roughness and SCS Curve Number grids. Manning's n is assigned from USGS Annual NLCD land cover and impervious fraction (n_paved = 0.030, n_water = 0.020, n_open_space = 0.035, n_vegetation = 0.060). SCS Curve Numbers (CN ∈ [30, 100]; USDA NRCS, 2004a, NEH-630 Ch. 9) combine NLCD classes with U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) Soil Survey Geographic Database (SSURGO) Hydrologic Soil Groups, defaulting urban fill and unclassified cells to group D.

Governing Equations & Subgrid Formulation

Deltares SFINCS solves the 2D local-inertial shallow-water equations (Bates et al., 2010) on a staggered Arakawa C-grid with simplified upwind advection enabled (advection = 1), horizontal eddy viscosity enabled (viscosity = 1), wetting/drying threshold huthresh = 0.01 m, and Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) stability factor α = 0.75 (Leijnse et al., 2021; van Ormondt et al., 2025). Mass conservation and x-direction momentum conservation (with an analogous y-direction equation) are:

∂ζ∂t + ∂qx∂x + ∂qy∂y = Rnet − ISCS
(1)
∂qx∂t + ∂(u qx)∂x + ∂(v qx)∂y + g h ∂ζ∂x + g n2 |q| qxh7/3 − ν h ∇2 qx = 0
(2)

where ζ is water-surface elevation (m NAVD88), q = (qx, qy) is unit discharge (m2/s), h is flow depth (m), u and v are depth-averaged velocities, g is gravitational acceleration, n is Manning's roughness, ν is eddy viscosity, Rnet is net precipitation after storm-drain abstraction, and ISCS is spatially distributed SCS Curve Number soil infiltration (scsfile). For citywide San Francisco, a 20 m computational grid (650 × 620 cells, 329,070 active) stores pre-computed subgrid volume–depth and wet-area conveyance lookup tables (sfincs.sbg with nr_subgrid_pixels = 10, nbins = 10, and max_gradient = 5.0; van Ormondt et al., 2025). Peak coarse water-surface elevations (zsmax) are bilinearly downscaled onto the 2 m hydro-enforced DEM, and added compound SLR depth is isolated as:

d2m = max(0, zsmax2m − dem2m),      ΔdSLR = max(0, d(Storm + SLR) − d(Storm + 0 ft))
(3)

During v0.6 calibration, an antecedent soil moisture test evaluated 120-hour prior NOAA MRMS rainfall against the legacy SCS dormant-season wet threshold (> 28.0 mm / 1.1 in; USDA SCS, 1972; Chow et al., 1988), converting standard CNII to wet CNIII (Chow et al., 1988):

CNIII = 23 · CNII 10 + 0.13 · CNII
(4)

Note that USDA NRCS (2004b, NEH-630 Ch. 10) subsequently replaced rainfall-based Antecedent Moisture Condition tables with Antecedent Runoff Condition (ARC) and no longer endorses fixed 5-day rainfall thresholds.

Storm-Drain Abstraction & Compound Forcing

Because uniform 1D pipe-invert data are unavailable across the nine-county Bay Area, subsurface storm-drain removal is structured into four progressive drainage tiers (distinct from validation Tiers A–E below):

  • Tier 0 (Uniform scalar abstraction; v0.6 default). Subtracts a constant 5.0 mm/hr from rainfall over land while retaining spatially distributed scsfile soil infiltration, following national surface-water screening practice (Environment Agency, 2021, 2025). Here, 5.0 mm/hr is an effective (calibrated) parameter rather than physical pipe capacity; the 2.5–20.0 mm/hr sensitivity sweep and comparisons with UK Environment Agency defaults and SFPUC's Level of Service storm are detailed in Sensitivity Analyses (Table 8).
  • Tier 1a (Imperviousness-scaled 2D drainage). Extends the design-standard drainage concept of Wing et al. (2017), who scaled drainage capacity from night-time luminosity, by parameterizing spatially varying drainage from NLCD imperviousness via SFINCS netamprfile, tested both un-matched (2–10 mm/hr in v0.4) and mean-rate-matched (scaled so the urban-land mean equals 5.0 mm/hr in v0.6).
  • Tier 1b (Tide-attenuated 2D drainage). Extends Tier 1a by linearly attenuating gravity-outfall capacity as (z − tide(t)) / 1.5 m approaches zero outside levee-protected pumped polders.
  • Tier 2 (Municipal GIS enrichment; planned v0.7+). Incorporates open municipal storm-drain, culvert, and pump-station GIS where available in separate-MS4 watersheds.

Shoreline boundary cells (elevation ≤ 0.5 m NAVD88) are driven by four National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Center for Operational Oceanographic Products and Services (CO-OPS) tide gauges: Presidio (9414290), Alameda (9414750), Richmond (9414863), and Redwood City (9414523), while upland cells along the southern San Mateo County border (> 0.5 m NAVD88) act as open outflow boundaries (msk = 3). The four-gauge NOAA CO-OPS spatial interpolation is this project's own boundary-forcing choice, capturing San Francisco Bay's +0.34 m Mean Higher High Water (MHHW) amplification and +60 min phase lag toward the South Bay (Nederhoff et al., 2021). Coincident stress scenarios combine 24-hour NOAA Atlas 14 Vol. 6 precipitation depths (90.4 mm for the 10-year storm; 144.0 mm for the 100-year storm; Perica et al., 2011; rev. 2014) distributed via 15-minute alternating-block hyetographs (Chow et al., 1988) with semidiurnal tides (1.85 m MHHW plus 0.35 m storm surge) and 0, +1, +3, or +5 ft SLR. Because rain–surge dependence is not jointly modeled, the joint return period of these coincident stress scenarios is undefined (Wahl et al., 2015; Moftakhari et al., 2017; Gori & Lin, 2022; cf. Bates et al., 2021), and they must not be interpreted as a joint "100-year flood." Historical AR hindcasts use hourly 1 km NOAA Multi-Radar Multi-Sensor (MRMS) Quantitative Precipitation Estimation (QPE) radar-gauge rainfall (rain_source = "mrms"; Zhang et al., 2016) and observed four-gauge tides.

Multi-Tier QA, Validation & Holdout Protocol

All pluvial metrics are computed strictly over valid land (elevation ≥ 0.5 m NAVD88, excluding NLCD open water) across one boundary-condition QA tier (Tier A) and four independent open-data validation tiers (Tiers B–E):

  • Tier A (Boundary-condition QA check). NOAA CO-OPS tide gauges scored with root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), peak timing error, and Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE; Grimley et al., 2025). Presidio (9414290) is one of the four forcing gauges and the only gauge inside the San Francisco domain ([542600..555600, 4173000..4185400]); Alameda, Richmond, and Redwood City lie 6–26 km outside. Tier A therefore checks boundary-condition application rather than independent skill, and leave-one-gauge-out validation is deferred to a multi-gauge domain (v0.7+/v1.0).
  • Tier B (Authoritative hydraulic benchmark). San Francisco Public Utilities Commission (SFPUC) 100-Year Storm Flood Risk Map evaluated at ≥ 0.1524 m (6 in) via Hit Rate (HR), False Alarm Ratio (FAR), and Critical Success Index (CSI), following local California benchmark comparisons in Sanders et al. (2023) and Schubert et al. (2024). Because CSI is sensitive to event size and biased toward overprediction and low-gradient terrain (Stephens et al., 2014), the evaluation applies a like-for-like "deep and contiguous" connected-area filter (≥ 0.1524 m over ≥ 5,574 m2, half an average SF city block; SFPUC, 2024), scores across 0/+1/+3/+5 ft SLR, and reports continuous depth diagnostics.
  • Tier C (Roadway and emergency disruptions). DataSF Department of Emergency Management traffic hazard dispatches and San Francisco Fire Department (SFFD) water rescue calls (310 points across 5 ARs), scored at ≥ 0.05 m (2 in) with 25 m and 50 m capture rates, lift (the ratio of the flooded report rate to the flooded control rate), and Mann–Whitney receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC). Using crowdsourced and dispatch reports follows precedents such as Praharaj et al. (2021); the evaluation reports target-group right-of-way controls (streets and sidewalks; Phillips et al., 2009) alongside valid-land controls.
  • Tier D (Civic reports and news archives). DataSF 311 flood and sewer reports (2,071 valid-land points across 5 ARs) scored at ≥ 0.05 m (2 in) with 0, 25, and 50 m capture, lift over 2,000 seeded random controls (both valid-land and target-group right-of-way controls; Phillips et al., 2009), ROC-AUC (Agonafir et al., 2022; Lerma et al., 2025), and census-tract median-income tercile stratification to diagnose socioeconomic reporting bias (Kontokosta & Hong, 2021; Boxer et al., 2025). Google Research Groundsource (332 small-footprint records across 41 San Francisco sub-basins) is also audited with a full confusion matrix and specificity.
  • Tier E (Structural loss cross-check). OpenFEMA National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) redacted claims (259 San Francisco census tracts, 92 claims, $1.54M paid) tested via Spearman rank correlation (ρ) and ties-aware Kendall τb against modeled flooded land area and exposed buildings (Wing et al., 2020), alongside explicit reporting of zero-claim tract share.

Following Klemeš's (1986) split-sample testing hierarchy, the five-storm ensemble was partitioned into three calibration storms (Oct 2021, Dec 2022, and Jan 2023 ARs; 1,394 DataSF 311 points) and two untouched holdout storms (Feb 2024 and Nov 2024 ARs; 677 points total). Candidate physics changes were tested one factor at a time on the calibration storms and the 100-year 0 ft SLR coincident stress scenario. A change was retained only if it improved at least two independent calibration tiers without regressing SFPUC CSI and cleared a parsimony threshold (≥ +0.01 ROC-AUC or ≥ +0.005 CSI). Holdout storms are excluded from model selection and evaluated only after selection is complete; v0.6 selection was unaffected because no candidate passed the calibration criteria.

Baseline Evaluation, Rescored Diagnostics & Holdout Storms

Across all five ARs (2,071 valid-land 311 points and 310 Tier C dispatches; Tables 4 and 5), the frozen v0.6 baseline captures flooding well during the four heavy storms and scores near chance on the moderate February 2024 holdout. On the November 2024 holdout (70.3 mm mean MRMS rainfall, 536 311 reports), 0 m capture is 11.19% (3.928× valid-land lift; 2.573× right-of-way lift) and 25 m capture is 57.09% (1.749× valid-land lift, 0.6419 AUC; 1.524× right-of-way lift, 0.6125 AUC). On the February 2024 holdout (54.7 mm mean, 141 311 reports), 0 m capture is 0.00%, 25 m capture is 0.71% (0.268× valid-land lift, 0.4980 AUC; 0.373× right-of-way lift, 0.5057 AUC), and 25 m Tier C capture is 1.03%. Switching from valid-land controls to street and sidewalk right-of-way controls (Phillips et al., 2009) raises the pooled 25 m control flooded rate from 28.43% to 31.86% and lowers pooled 25 m 311 lift from 1.698× to 1.516× (AUC 0.6247 to 0.6004).

Sensitivity Analyses

To test sensitivity to the uniform Tier 0 drainage rate without altering the frozen v0.6 baseline, a 2.5–20.0 mm/hr sweep was run across the 100-year 0 ft SLR coincident stress scenario and the three calibration storms (Oct 2021, Dec 2022, Jan 2023; 16 new SFINCS runs plus the existing 5.0 mm/hr runs; Table 8). SFPUC's SSIP Level of Service storm is the 5-year, 3-hour storm (1.28 in / 32.5 mm total, peak 1-hour depth 0.72 in / 18.3 mm; derived from a 1941 City intensity–duration–frequency curve; San Francisco Public Works, 2017, Sec. 3.1 & Table 3), implying a mean rate of 10.84 mm/hr (~10.8 mm/hr) and a peak-hour rate of ~18.3 mm/hr—comparable to England's Environment Agency urban defaults (12 mm/hr in 2013, Environment Agency, 2021; 18 mm/hr in 2025, Environment Agency, 2025). Because the 1941 City IDF basis differs from the NOAA Atlas 14 basis of the design storms, this comparison is approximate. On hourly-averaged MRMS hindcasts and the 100-year 0 ft benchmark, ranking skill (AUC and CSI) is flat across 2.5–5.0 mm/hr (differences below the +0.01 AUC and +0.005 CSI parsimony thresholds), while 311 capture is ~12 percentage points higher at 2.5 mm/hr (61.62% vs. 49.71%; AUC 0.6084 vs. 0.6054) and SFPUC filtered CSI (0.2344 vs. 0.2331) and Tier C AUC (0.5876 vs. 0.5832) are slightly higher at 5.0 mm/hr. Above ~10 mm/hr, pluvial hindcast skill collapses (311 capture drops to 7.89% at 10.0 mm/hr and 0.29% at 15.0–20.0 mm/hr, even though Tier E NFIP area τb ticks up to 0.1841–0.1924). Thus, 5.0 mm/hr is retained as the frozen v0.6 effective (calibrated) parameter rather than a demonstrated optimum or physical pipe capacity, sitting well below both the LOS mean (~10.8 mm/hr) and LOS peak-hour (~18.3 mm/hr) rates because hourly MRMS averaging smooths short high-intensity bursts.

Discussion

Why Uniform Tier 0 Outperformed Spatially Scaled Drainage

Uniform Tier 0 drainage (5.0 mm/hr) outperformed NLCD-imperviousness-scaled Tier 1a and Tier 1b in San Francisco, even after removing v0.4's over-abstraction through exact domain-mean rate matching. In San Francisco's combined-sewer core, the most flood-prone topographic bowls (SOMA, Alemany, and downtown) are also the most impervious cells (80–100% impervious). Scaling 2D drainage upward with imperviousness removes more than 5.0 mm/hr directly inside those bowls while lowering removal on pervious parks, starving street ponding and dropping SFPUC CSI from 0.1953 to 0.1689. Shifting to AMC-III uniformly increases runoff across pervious and semi-pervious soils, lifting 311 capture by 5.50 percentage points at the cost of broader false-positive road inundation (Tier C lift fell from 1.338× to 1.252×, and Tier C AUC fell by 0.0044). In the 2.5–20.0 mm/hr drainage sweep (Table 8), ranking skill (AUC, CSI) is flat across 2.5–5.0 mm/hr while 311 capture is higher at 2.5 mm/hr, and pluvial hindcast skill collapses above ~10 mm/hr; 5.0 mm/hr is retained as the frozen v0.6 effective parameter rather than a demonstrated optimum or physical pipe capacity.

The two holdout storms behave very differently. During the Nov 2024 AR (70.3 mm MRMS mean with intense convective bursts), modeled ponding reaches 57.09% 25 m capture (1.524× right-of-way lift, 0.6125 AUC; 1.749× valid-land lift, 0.6419 AUC) and is included in the web viewer. On the Feb 2024 AR (54.7 mm mean, 141 311 reports), the model scores at chance: 0.71% 25 m capture (0.373× right-of-way lift, 0.5057 AUC; 0.268× valid-land lift, 0.4980 AUC). Rainfall during that event stayed mostly below 5.0 mm/hr in the hourly MRMS product, which can smooth out shorter street-ponding bursts, so reported 311 and traffic incidents likely reflect clogged catch basins and wind-downed trees rather than widespread overland ponding.

Limitations & Model Boundaries

Two forcing and parameter-transfer uncertainties directly affect the synthetic stress scenarios. First, the 5.0 mm/hr effective drainage rate was evaluated on hourly (60-minute) MRMS hindcasts but is applied to 15-minute alternating-block design storms, so design-storm flood extents may be biased wet relative to the hindcast evaluation. Second, modeled extents are highly sensitive to assumed storm shape (Table 9): replacing the 15-minute alternating block with the NOAA Atlas 14 Vol. 6 50% median temporal distribution cuts the 100-year 0 ft SLR flooded area (≥ 0.05 m) by ~44% (7.373 to 4.160 km2) and roughly halves SFPUC filtered CSI (0.2344 to 0.1184). Because the Atlas 14 median curve smooths individual storm peaks, it represents a low-intensity bound rather than the "correct" storm shape, and design-storm depths should be interpreted accordingly.

Flat-street nuisance ponding under uniform Tier 0 in separate-MS4 flatlands will be re-tested in v0.7 using mean-rate-matched Tier 1a/1b drainage and optional Tier 2 municipal GIS inputs. Pumped coastal polders (such as East Palo Alto, Foster City, and Bay Farm Island) will incorporate explicit polder_mask_source exclusions and SFINCS pump structures, and open-coast Pacific wave setup and runup at Ocean Beach can be coupled through SFINCS SnapWave (Leijnse et al., 2021).

Without complete 1D pipe-invert elevations and hydraulic grade lines across 101 Bay Area jurisdictions, subsurface trunk surcharging, individual clogged catch basins, and uphill lateral backups cannot be explicitly resolved in a 2D-only model. Although simplified upwind advection is enabled, SFINCS's local-inertial formulation is not a shock-capturing full shallow-water solver. As shown by de Almeida & Bates (2013), the local-inertial equations agree well with the full shallow-water equations for Froude numbers below roughly 0.5, with growing errors (milder depth gradients and slower wave propagation) as Froude number rises; consequently, supercritical sheetflow on steep hillsides (such as Twin Peaks or Bernal Heights) remains approximate (Leijnse et al., 2021; van Ormondt et al., 2025).

Four additional physical and statistical limitations are not yet represented in the current model and are planned to be addressed in future updates:

  • Groundwater rise under SLR. Rising sea levels elevate coastal water tables, reducing soil infiltration capacity (Curve Number term) and infiltrating combined sewers to reduce effective drainage (Befus et al., 2020; Plane et al., 2019; Bosserelle et al., 2022). Current +SLR runs hold both infiltration and drainage constant.
  • Land subsidence & vertical land motion. Sea level rise is applied as a spatially uniform offset, omitting local subsidence and variable vertical land motion around San Francisco Bay (Shirzaei & Bürgmann, 2018; Govorčin et al., 2025).
  • Joint rain–surge probability. Coincident stress scenarios (10-yr/100-yr rainfall paired with MHHW + 0.35 m surge) do not model statistical rain–surge dependence or empirical joint return periods (Wahl et al., 2015; Moftakhari et al., 2017; Gori & Lin, 2022).
  • Outfall & treatment capacity under SLR. Coastal combined-sewer outfall and wastewater treatment capacity constraints under rising tailwater are not explicitly resolved (Hummel et al., 2018).

Uncalibrated Spatial Transfer (v0.7 to v1.0)

In the next development cycle (v0.7), the frozen v0.6 defaults will be run unmodified as an uncalibrated spatial transfer baseline (a Klemeš, 1986 proxy-basin test) on the San Francisquito Creek and East Palo Alto separate-MS4 watershed, validating against USGS stream gauge 11164500 and county benchmarks before adding pumped-polder structures or separate-MS4 drainage tests. This establishes a watershed-based parameterization without county-line seams ahead of the nine-county v1.0 regional mosaic.
